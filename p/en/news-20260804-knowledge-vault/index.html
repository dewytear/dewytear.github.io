The Man Who Records Everything, and the Wiki That Publishes — Reflections on Knowledge Vaults

An essay that dissects a lifelogger's podcast transcript, places it side by side with this wiki, and draws out where we should go next.

Where It Started — Three Questions

This piece began with three questions about this wiki itself. Is it closer to RAG, to an LLM wiki, or can it be called a second brain? The answers settled as follows — RAG is a pipeline (a way of consuming) that retrieves from a corpus at question time and feeds generation, while this wiki is the canonical knowledge source that such a pipeline consumes: “not RAG, but RAG-ready.” Its operation shares a skeleton with the LLM wiki Karpathy proposed (a knowledge base written by AI and curated by a human), but it diverges in being a public publication rather than a private vault, with a dual interface — a human-facing UI plus a machine interface for AIs (llms.txt & knowledge-graph.json). On the five-level second-brain ladder, it sits around level 3–4: capture, organize, connect, search.

As an extension of those questions, I received the transcript of a conversation with someone who practices the same subject at the opposite density. Brian, an AX consultant who runs the YouTube channel “Brain Trinity,” demonstrated his recording system on a podcast: a necklace recorder capturing 24 hours of audio, Screenpipe capturing 24 hours of screen, everything flowing into Obsidian vaults for AI-assisted retrospectives and planning — a self-described “recording maniac” system. This essay dissects that conversation, compares it with this wiki on equal footing, and lays out where we go next.

Dissecting the Conversation — Three Layers of Brian's System

The system demonstrated in the podcast resolves into three layers.

① Capture layer — always-on automatic collection. A necklace recorder (Omi) records audio all day, splitting events at two minutes of silence and piling transcriptions into Obsidian; Screenpipe records the screen 24/7 while a local model summarizes every 30 minutes. Meetings go through a dedicated recorder (PLAUD); web material lands via the Obsidian Web Clipper, full transcripts included; diaries are dictated by STT before bed. The point is that the willpower cost of capture has been driven to zero — he never decides whether to record.

② Structure layer — a five-layer daily note and two vaults. Each day is recorded in five strata: intent (planner) → execution (transcripts & time-stamped logs) → reality (screen recordings — the parts words never captured) → interpretation → AI logs. Keeping plan (intent) and measurement (execution & reality) separate turns retrospectives into a “reconciliation of accounts.” His vaults split in two — one for light consumption, one for deep engagement. Material enters a raw folder as-is; passing through ingest, concepts (entities) are extracted, cross-linked, and reconstituted as refined notes in a wiki folder. Two moves stand out: defining ingest not as Markdown conversion but as connection and indexing, and wiring the system to ask back, at ingest time, “why did you capture this? how do you want to use it later?” He calls this “making the unconscious conscious.”

③ Use layer — automated retrospectives and agents. At 2 a.m. daily, AI produces a retrospective from the day's full data; weekly, monthly, quarterly, and yearly reviews stack on top. He once received a year-long narrative “exactly as it felt in his head,” and famously pulled a love story out of his dating-era records and used it to propose — an anecdote that sums up the system's emotional payoff. The entry point is a Telegram agent (Hermes); the next goal is a Discord “advisory board” of mentor personas.

Two pipelines from the same lineage (Karpathy's LLM wiki) — the top treats a whole life, privately; the bottom treats knowledge, publicly.

Same Lineage, Different Choices — A Point-by-Point Comparison

The two systems speak a strikingly similar vocabulary: raw versus refined (his raw/wiki, our documents/index), concept extraction and linking at ingest (his entities, our concepts & relations), accumulated logs (his AI logs and slash-log, our work log), and the maxim that “an ontology is a document, not a graph” — he keeps his worldview, direction, and knowledge architecture as documents and treats them as the ordering principle, which is exactly the role played here by the knowledge-system (kgs) document set. Three Arguments the Conversation Raises

Argument 1 — RAG versus LLM wiki is a question of when you refine. The podcast summarizes the difference in two points: RAG pays embedding, chunking, and re-indexing costs every time documents grow, while an LLM wiki only needs to connect new documents incrementally; and RAG's vectors are unreadable to humans, while an LLM wiki stays human-readable Markdown, so people and AI share one canon. Translated into this wiki's terms — RAG defers refinement to query time; an LLM wiki finishes refinement at ingest time. This wiki's entry pipeline (concept extraction, relation review, translation, index regeneration) is the extreme of the latter, which is precisely what makes it possible to consume knowledge-graph.json by traversing a single file as-is. But the distinction is a division of layers, not a rivalry — putting semantic search (RAG-like) on top of a refined wiki is what levels 3 and 4 of the five-level ladder look like together, and that is in fact how this wiki's unified search works.

Argument 2 — the cause of failure is not the tool but the absence of purpose. The sharpest observation in the podcast concerns why the second-brain boom mass-produced vaults that were built but never used — tombs of knowledge: purposeless adoption and no prior experience of structuring oneself. His remedy is to make the system ask back at ingest: “why did you capture this? where do you want to use it?” — a device that records motive as data at the moment of collection. What is striking is that this wiki already encodes the same principle at a different layer: relations may only be declared when they actually exist in the text, forced links are forbidden, and concepts must appear in the body (the honesty rule); isolated documents are treated as review signals, not defects. The moment connecting and collecting become the goal, the vault dies — he implements that insight as a question, we implement it as gates and rules.

Argument 3 — an ontology is a document, not a graph. The podcast distinguishes ontology (order) from the knowledge graph (an instance of that order), and locates the ontology's substance in documents that say “who I am and how I think.” This wiki does exactly that — the World → Domain → System → Document hierarchy, the five-type relation vocabulary, and the rule that concepts are join keys all live as prose in the kgs documents, and the graph that appears in Graph Studio is an instance of that order. Keeping the schema in documents before code is also what lets an AI read the rules and follow them when it works on the repository.

Where We Go Next — This Wiki's Next Steps

What this wiki should take from the comparison is not “record more.” Given its identity as a public publication, always-on capture of a whole life is not its path — and the podcast itself draws the line: “not everyone needs to do this; being able to retrieve when needed is enough.” Four things are worth taking.

  • Make the motive explicit — his “why did you capture this?” question can be grafted onto our entry procedure. Today a document's “why” is scattered across work logs; recording a one-line capture motive in doc-entries at entry time would improve later retrieval.
  • Automate periodic retrospectives — work logs accumulate daily, but there is no monthly or quarterly review. Like his 2 a.m. retrospective, a scheduled job that drafts a monthly review from work logs, commits, and the backlog is the cheapest first step toward level 5 (an always-on brain OS).
  • A lightweight inbox layer — like his raw/wiki split, this wiki has no layer for “material before the value judgment.” The entry bar (three languages, relations, gates) is high enough that good material can be lost; a private, non-published inbox beside the wiki, promoting only what matures, is worth considering.
  • A conversational entry point — today AI consumption is left to whoever reads the exports. A QA entry point that knows the wiki, like his Telegram agent, would extend consumption into dialogue while leaving the canon untouched.

In one line — he is building a system that records a life and gives it back to its owner; this wiki is building a system that refines knowledge and publishes it to everyone, human and AI. Two extremes of one lineage, with clear things to learn from each other: from him, motive-as-data and automated retrospectives; from this wiki, what he might take is machine discipline and the sustainability of public publication. A knowledge vault succeeds or fails not by tools or density, but by whether purpose is engraved into the structure — that is the conclusion of this comparison.
