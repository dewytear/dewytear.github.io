The End of Coding by Hand? — The Future of Developers as Seen by SNU's Prof. Jaewook Lee

“In the future humans will no longer need to code with their own fingers — do you agree with that proposition?” That is the question SNU Catch Season 2, the knowledge-talk program on Seoul National University's official channel (hosted by Sungwoon Choi), put to Prof. Jaewook Lee of the Department of Computer Science and Engineering at SNU (director of the SNU AI Institute, researching AI infrastructure). His answer is unexpectedly plain — “The part where you write code can be automated to nearly 100%. But defining what to build remains the domain of humans.” This article organizes the core argument of the talk and reinforces the claims it makes with figures and sources cross-checked through web search.

Three-line summary

  • Prof. Jaewook Lee believes “writing code itself will be nearly 100% automated.” What remains is defining what to build, and reading and critiquing the result — in other words, development turns into ‘the work of a manager who manages agents’.
  • That makes the central issue in education “students becoming couriers” — if they simply pass the professor's problem to an AI and bring back only the answer, no learning happens. Curricula have to shift toward training students to define problems themselves and to critique results.
  • On the job outlook, his diagnosis is that reality lies somewhere between “the pie gets bigger” (the Jevons paradox) and “demand disappears” — but the rising bar for entry-level hires is something actually happening right now, and external data confirms it (verified below).

Just three terms to know first

TermPlain explanation
vibe codingBuilding software by telling it what to do in words and just checking the result instead of writing code line by line. AI researcher Andrej Karpathy coined the name in February 2025, describing it as “forgetting that the code even exists and giving in to the vibes.” Earlier, in January 2023, he left the famous line “The hottest new programming language is English.”
the Jevons paradoxAn observation by the economist Jevons in 1865 — when steam engines became more coal-efficient, coal consumption did not fall but exploded. When something gets cheaper, the places to use it multiply. It is used as an argument that “if coding gets easier, the total amount of coding and the demand for development may actually grow,” and Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella quoted it in early 2025 with “Jevons paradox strikes again!”
capability overhangThe gap in which a model's capabilities race ahead while people's skill at using them grows far more slowly. It is a concept Prof. Lee raised in the talk, and it explains why the productivity gap between power users and average users widens so much even with the same tool.

“Implementation gets automated — what remains is definition and critique”

The change Prof. Lee himself feels as a researcher who codes is interesting. In the past most of his energy went into ‘writing things’ — papers, code, slides — whereas now he spends most of his time ‘reading and critiquing’. Turning an idea into something concrete has become easy, and what matters now is the ability to read what was built critically.

So his answer to the question “will we stop coding by hand?” splits in two — the share of automation in implementation will keep rising and may reach nearly 100%, but defining what to implement and what you are trying to achieve remains the human part. He likens this way of working to “a manager's job.” Working with AI is like managing a team: you have to divide roles among agents and judge the results — and just as it is hard to be a good engineering manager without hands-on engineering experience, if you have never coded yourself you cannot delegate properly either.

Automation keeps expanding in the implementation stretch (the middle), but definition and critique at either end remain human work — Prof. Jaewook Lee's core argument.

Why developers of all people became the first target

Predictions that “AI will replace white-collar work” were common, but not many people expected it to start ‘with developers’. Prof. Lee sums up the reasons in three points.

  • The early adopters were the developers themselves — the group that used AI first and most heavily was software developers.
  • Plenty of data, and a logical structure — a vast amount of code data has piled up on the internet, and code has a clear logical structure that makes it easy for AI to learn.
  • It actually performed well — people tried it and it worked, which set off a cycle of using it even more.

External figures back this up. Google CEO Sundar Pichai said on the October 2024 earnings call that “more than 25% of Google's new code is AI-generated,” then more than 30% in April 2025, and in a recent announcement said it had risen to 75% (landed after engineer review and approval).

The power of vibe coding — and the 95% trap

A real case Prof. Lee cited in the talk is striking. A civil servant at the Gwangjin-gu district office, thinking “administrative regulations are too complicated, let's have AI look them up,” built a chatbot-style system that cross-references the rules and answers questions — vibe coding will take you all the way to a prototype that affects real work, even without formal development training.

But the bar for ‘finished software’ is different. Code that works 95% of the time is effectively broken by professional product standards, and even 99% can be a loose bar — in a service used by a billion people, a 1% malfunction rate means 10 million people get a wrong result. In finance or security it goes without saying. This is also where the warning comes from that having built something with vibe coding can lead to overestimating your own skill.

This ‘gap between perception and measurement’ has been confirmed by experiment as well. In a randomized controlled trial by the AI evaluation organization METR (July 2025), 16 experienced open-source developers were given 246 real tasks, and the side using AI tools was actually 19% slower — while the developers themselves perceived that they had been “20% faster.” (METR itself attached the caveat that these are “historical results based on the tools of the time,” but the lesson that judging productivity by feel alone can be wrong still holds.) This lines up exactly with the capability overhang Prof. Lee pointed to — the gap where the tools race ahead while skill at using them lags behind. He names two conditions for “using AI well”: knowing what it can accomplish (the category of task), and knowing what it cannot (its limits).

Education — the student who became a ‘courier’

As an educator, Prof. Lee's worry is concrete. Textbook-style assignments have run out of life as learning tools because AI now solves them with 100% accuracy, and worse is the arrangement in which the student becomes a ‘middleman’ — the professor sets a problem, and the student feeds it to an AI, receives the answer, and submits it. Neither the making nor the solving of the problem was the student's, so there is no place left for learning to happen. In his words, the student ends up playing the role of a “glorified courier.”

The ‘middleman (courier)’ problem Prof. Jaewook Lee described — when someone else does both the defining and the solving, no learning is left in the student's seat.

He offers a two-track solution as well.

  • Keep teaching the basic concepts — the same way we do not stop teaching arithmetic just because calculators exist. To interpret and evaluate a result you have a threshold of knowledge to clear, and the basics beneath it are checked in traditional ways such as exams and quizzes.
  • Hand problem definition over to the students — train them with open-ended assignments that give only minimal constraints and say “design it yourself.” Beyond that, redesign the whole curriculum to be AI-native — assume the use of AI tools in every course, raise the weight of design and problem framing, and add the experience of actually shipping a project and sessions where students critique each other's code and designs.

He sees the same direction on the job side — instead of the arrangement where planners hand tasks down from above and developers take them and implement them, the ‘PM-developer hybrid’ who does everything from planning to development is on the rise. In fact, he adds, if you look at recent AI competition winners, many of them are people who deeply understand the problems of a particular domain (an industry or a job). In the end, value comes from choosing well “which problem to solve.”

Jobs — the Jevons paradox, the lesson of the ATM, and the bar for new hires

Two extremes face off in the outlook. One side is the Jevons paradox — if coding gets easier, the total amount of coding and the industry's pie grow, so demand rises. The other side holds that AI will fully automate coding and demand for human coding will vanish. Prof. Lee's diagnosis is that “reality is somewhere in between.”

The story he cited about ATMs and bank tellers is history confirmed by actual research. According to an analysis by the economist James Bessen, the number of US bank tellers did not shrink during the spread of ATMs but actually grew — as the tellers needed per branch fell from 21 to 13, running a branch got cheaper, and banks increased urban branches by 43%, which raised total demand for tellers. Tellers' work shifted from handling cash to consulting and selling financial products. (It is also telling that teller employment only actually bent downward after mobile banking in the 2010s — a more complete automation that removed the branch visit itself.)

That said, the data is clear on the rising bar for entry-level hires that Prof. Lee named as “something actually happening right now.” When Stanford researchers (Brynjolfsson et al.) analyzed data from ADP, the largest payroll processor in the US, they found that after the spread of generative AI employment of 22- to 25-year-olds in occupations with high AI exposure fell 13% in relative terms, and in particular employment of 22- to 25-year-old software developers fell about 20% from its late-2022 peak through July 2025. As for why younger workers are hit harder, the researchers offered the interpretation that “what new entrants learned in school overlaps most with what LLMs are already good at” — which maps precisely onto the argument in the talk.

“If you were twenty again, would you still study computer science?”

To the host's mischievous question — “if you were 20 today, entering in the class of 2026, would you choose computer science again?” — Prof. Lee answers “yes.” The reason is interesting. The change AI brought is equally new to veterans and newcomers alike, so the starting line has become fair (in the phrase quoted in the talk, “The Great Equalizer”), and a wider space than ever has opened up in which anyone with a good idea can lead an innovation.

Finally, his answer to “why study computer science even so” goes back to the definition of the discipline — computer science is not a department that teaches coding, but one that teaches a way of thinking that abstracts complex problems into a form you can solve. Concretely, that means algorithmic thinking, which breaks complicated work into simple steps, and data-driven thinking, which draws insight out of data distributions — two frames that will keep being needed on the side that judges the results, no matter how far AI advances. The conclusion of the talk is that the less code we write, the more paradoxically the value of a discipline that builds frames of thought may grow.

Fact-check note

  • Remarks from the talk are distinguished from external facts. The Gwangjin-gu chatbot case, the statistics mentioned in connection with capability overhang, the “nearly 100% automation” outlook, and so on are marked as Prof. Jaewook Lee's remarks in the talk, and every item in the table of figures was cross-checked against independent sources.
  • Two Karpathy quotes verified — “The hottest new programming language is English” comes from a tweet on 2023-01-24, and ‘vibe coding’ originates in a tweet in February 2025.
  • Context for Google's 75% figure — it is a company-announced figure on the basis of “code generated by AI and reviewed and approved by engineers,” not an independently audited number. The 25% (2024-10) and 30% (2025-04) figures are likewise remarks from earnings calls.
  • The shelf life of METR's result — METR later stated explicitly that these are “historical results based on the tools and workflows of early 2025.” This article cites it only for the methodological lesson about the ‘gap between perception and measurement’.
