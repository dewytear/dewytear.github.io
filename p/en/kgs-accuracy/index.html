How Often Does This Wiki Get It Right — Measuring Search and Graph Traversal

This wiki introduces itself as a "RAG-ready knowledge source," opens a machine-readable layer to AI, and ships its engine as a plugin. Behind every one of those claims, there has never been a measured accuracy number. This page publishes the first ones, good and bad together.

Three-line summary

  • Search finds words and misses sentences. Two or three content words almost always put the right page first, but ask in a full sentence and all 20 Korean questions return nothing at all.
  • The related block is mostly right when you follow it. Of 252 edges, 13.5% lead nowhere useful. But of the paths authors drew themselves in the text, the related block lays down only a third within one hop.
  • The bottleneck is the entrance. On questions that need two pages, once search finds one of them, the related block supplies the other three times out of four. When search finds neither, the graph never gets a chance.

Why measure now

Two things happened the same week. On 23 September Anthropic widened Claude Marketplace into a catalog of about 2,000 connectors, plugins, products and implementation partners. It had launched on 6 March with six partners; enterprise customers can now spend existing commitments on third-party tools and receive a single invoice. Plugins have become products on a shelf.

The same day, Karpathy's LLM Wiki gist (the pattern in which an LLM builds and queries a markdown wiki directly, without a vector database) drew a comment asking whether anyone had benchmarked the claims about why it works. That week implementers started posting numbers. One measured precision over 1,536 queries and reported 0.3086 on its fast tier; another reported 94.0% on the long-term memory benchmark LongMemEval against 71.2% for the comparison.

Both point at the same moment. When a tool you built for yourself is handed to others, "it works" only carries weight once it becomes a number. This wiki is an instance of that pattern and ships its engine as a plugin. The question applies to it directly.

What was measured, and how

Search ran the real code. A headless browser opened this wiki's index.html, waited for the knowledge index and the full-text body index to load, then called the same searchDocs() function the search box calls. Not a re-implementation — the function the screen uses. It was measured separately on the Korean, English and Japanese screens.

The query set was frozen before any results were seen. It has 107 queries of four kinds: one or two words typed into a search box, two or three content words with no particles, natural-language sentences, and questions whose answer spans two pages. Each query lists the page that answers it as the gold answer; where two pages qualify, hitting either counts. The limit is plain: the queries were written by someone who knows the pages, so their vocabulary sits closer to the text than a real reader's would. The high content-word scores should be read as an upper bound.

The graph was judged against lines authors drew. The "Related" block under each page (the same related an AI follows in knowledge-graph.json) is computed only from the rarity of shared concepts. Links authors wrote into the text, and the typed relations people declared, play no part in that calculation, so they make an independent yardstick. The 165 edges backed by neither were judged one by one — by the AI that wrote this page, not by a person.

The tool, the set and the judgments are all in the repository: tools/eval/retrieval.mjs is the harness, retrieval-set.json the queries, edge-audit.json the judgments. Anyone can re-run it and get the same numbers, or change a judgment and get different ones.

The table splits in two. Type words and it is nearly perfect; ask in sentences and it nearly always fails. The cause is the search rule. The query is cut at spaces, and a page is listed only if every piece appears somewhere inside that one page. If a single piece appears nowhere, the whole query returns nothing.

"How does this wiki connect documents?" as the search actually splits it on the English screen. Each number is how many of the 74 searchable pages contain that piece.

Among the 20 Korean sentences, 17 contained a dead piece like this — a verb ending with a question mark attached, the Korean equivalent of "documents?". But dead pieces are not the whole story. On a Korean example question about when skills are loaded, removing its dead ending still left four pieces that no single page shared, so it stayed at zero. The more pieces a query has, the fewer pages hold all of them.

A what-if tested dropping only the dead pieces and searching again — changing the input, not the engine. English sentences rose from 10% to 70% in the top eight: the example above, with the question mark gone, finds the right page at rank 2, because the remaining English words are common enough to share a page. Korean rose only from 0% to 15%, and Japanese from 10% to 20%. Japanese is written without spaces, so a whole sentence becomes a single piece and dies as a unit. In Korean, pieces with particles attached scatter across different pages and their intersection comes out empty.

Word search showed one weakness too. Type "harness" on the Korean screen and the defining page, What a Harness Is, comes in sixth. Eight pages carry "harness" as a concept, all eight tie at the top score of 8, and on a tie the newest page goes first. So each new news piece on the topic pushes the defining page one place further down. The score cannot tell a page about the topic from a page that brushes against it. The one English content-word query that missed came down to word form. It searched for "adoption," but the matching page is titled "Adopting Ontologies" and never uses "adoption" in its text. Without stemming, "adopting" and "adoption" are not substrings of each other.

Precision holds up. Pick an edge in a related block and follow it, and seven times in eight you land on a page that at least touches the topic. The 34 dead ends have three causes. Twenty come from concepts that are too common: when two words like plugin, install, skill or token cost overlap, an edge forms even across unrelated topics. Rarity weighting does make common concepts count for less, but the rule lets any pair sharing two or more concepts through, so two light ones together clear the bar. Ten are homonyms. "Metadata" meaning a note's front matter and "metadata" meaning a database's metadata views were filed under one concept key, and "retry" in agent cost and "retry" in message delivery the same. The last four are folder fill, where a page short of shared concepts is topped up with folder neighbours; in a folder with scattered topics, like the news folder, those neighbours were unrelated.

Recall is the weak side. Of 159 links where an author wrote "see also this" into the text, the related block lays down a third within one hop. For links that cross folders, it drops to a fifth. The related block is good at finding pages whose concepts overlap, but many of the connections an author thinks of never show up in the concept list. This is less a defect than two lines measuring different things: concept edges follow what a page is about; body links follow what a passage brings to mind. Still, an AI that traverses only through the related block will not see two thirds of the paths authors drew.

Joining the two — the bottleneck is the entrance

Twelve questions whose answer spans two pages joined search and traversal. Both pages landed in search's top three for only one question (8.3%), and widening to the top eight changed nothing. Adding the related blocks of those three pages raised it to three questions (25%).

Split by condition, the structure shows. For 4 of the 12 questions, search put at least one of the pair in its top three, and for three of those four the related block supplied the other. Given a foothold, the graph does its share. For the other 8, search missed both, and 7 of those returned nothing at all: four because a piece with a particle attached (the Korean for "and" glued to a noun) appeared nowhere, three because every piece appeared somewhere but no page held them all together. The graph is not weak; the door into it is narrow.

Why these numbers are not set beside other implementations'

It is tempting to line this page's figures up next to what LLM Wiki implementers posted — precision over 1,536 queries, accuracy on a long-term memory benchmark. This page does not. The tasks differ. They measured systems that store and recall memory on standard benchmarks; this page measured a published wiki's site search and related blocks on its own queries. One table would compare arrows shot at different targets. Their figures are also all self-reported.

The point here is not the size of the numbers but being on the side that publishes them. With the queries and judgments public, anyone can re-measure this wiki's claims the same way.

What could be fixed

This page measures and leaves the engine alone. The measurements point at these candidates.

  • Drop dead pieces and search again. When a search returns nothing, dropping pieces that match nowhere and retrying lifted English sentences from 10% to 70% in the what-if. It is the cheapest candidate with the largest effect.
  • Korean endings and particles; Japanese word boundaries. Korean needs endings like the question form and the "and" particle stripped, and Japanese needs sentences cut into words, before sentence search can work. Whether a list of common endings gets most of the way without full morphological analysis would have to be measured again.
  • Tie-breaking. On a tie between pages sharing a concept, preferring the page whose title contains the word, instead of the newest, would keep defining pages from sinking.
  • Concept hygiene. Of the 34 dead-end edges, 30 came from common concepts and homonyms. This wiki already flags over-broad concepts as review signals; this audit names exactly which concepts are causing trouble.

Whichever gets fixed, the same tool re-measures it. With the queries frozen, the before-and-after numbers are the effect. But fixing while looking at the queries tends to produce improvements tuned to those queries, so after a fix, adding fresh queries to check is the honest move.

Measurement conditions and fact-check note

Measured on 24 September 2026 against the wiki at commit fb6a831 (71 indexed pages, 74 searchable). This page was added after the measurement and is not in the results; once it becomes searchable, later results may shift slightly. The 107-query set was frozen before results were seen, and because it was written by someone who knows the pages, the content-word scores sit near an upper bound. The judgments on the 165 unbacked edges were made by the AI that wrote this page, not by a person. Search was run twice and gave identical numbers. The Claude Marketplace expansion (23 September, about 2,000 items, launched 6 March with six partners, purchases against existing commitments on a single invoice) was cross-checked with VentureBeat, The Next Web and the official page. The question on the LLM Wiki gist and the implementers' figures were confirmed in the gist's own comments; all figures are self-reported and no independent reproduction was found. The implementers are private individuals and are not named.
