How Often Does This Wiki Get It Right — Measuring Search and Graph Traversal

This wiki introduces itself as a "RAG-ready knowledge source," opens a machine-readable layer to AI, and ships its engine as a plugin. Claims like that need numbers behind them. This page measures how often the current site search and the related-pages block get it right, and gives what works and what does not the same weight.

Summary

  • Search is strong on words and mostly finds sentences too. One or two words, or a few content words, put the right page first almost every time. Asked as a sentence, the right page lands in the top eight 93.8% of the time in Korean and 75% in English and Japanese (on queries frozen in advance). No query came back empty.
  • The related block rarely leads nowhere. Of 199 edges, 4 (2.0%) point somewhere unrelated. But only a third of the links authors wrote into the text also appear in the related block, and only a seventh of the links that cross folders.
  • Three weak spots: conjugated Korean verbs, readers and pages using different words, and connections that cross folders.

Why measure

On 23 September Anthropic widened Claude Marketplace into a catalog of about 2,000 connectors, plugins, products and implementation partners. It had launched on 6 March with six partners; enterprise customers can now spend existing commitments on third-party tools and receive a single invoice. Plugins have become products on a shelf.

The same day, Karpathy's LLM Wiki gist (the pattern in which an LLM builds and queries a markdown wiki directly, without a vector database) drew a comment asking whether anyone had benchmarked the claims about why it works, and that week implementers started posting numbers. This wiki implements that pattern and ships its engine as a plugin. A tool handed to others needs its "it works" turned into a number.

How it was measured

Search ran the code the screen uses. A headless browser opened this wiki's index.html, waited for the knowledge index and the full-text body index, then called the same searchDocs() the search box calls. Each of the Korean, English and Japanese screens was measured separately.

There are two query sets. Each query lists the page that answers it as the gold answer; where two pages qualify, hitting either counts.

  • 48 frozen queries: 32 sentences, 8 one-or-two-word queries and 8 questions that need two pages. They were written and committed before the current search engine was built, so the engine could not be fitted to them. They supply this page's headline numbers.
  • 107 tuning queries: one or two words, content words without particles, sentences and two-page questions. They were frozen before any results were seen, but their failures were studied while the engine was refined, so their scores may run high. They appear for reference only.

Both sets were written by someone who knows the pages, so their vocabulary sits closer to the text than a real reader's would. This page quotes query sentences verbatim, so it was left out of both the search pool and the graph counts.

The graph was judged against lines authors drew. The "Related" block under each page (the same related an AI follows in knowledge-graph.json) is computed only from the rarity of shared concepts. Links authors wrote into the text and typed relations people declared play no part in it, so they make an independent yardstick. Edges backed by neither were judged one by one, by the AI that wrote this page and not by a person.

The harness, query sets and judgments are in the repository under tools/eval/ (retrieval.mjs, retrieval-set-fresh.json and retrieval-set.json, edge-audit.json). Re-running it gives the same numbers; changing a judgment gives different ones.

How search finds pages

The query is cut into pieces at spaces. Punctuation is stripped from each piece, and for Korean the forms left after cutting common endings and particles are searched too: a Korean question form of "connect" matches if its trimmed stem does, and "code" with a Korean particle glued on is searched as "code." English adds a stem with suffixes like -ing, -ation and -s removed. Japanese has no spaces, so only runs of katakana, kanji and Latin letters are kept, which cuts a sentence into words. Question words such as "how" are dropped. Endings are cut from a list, not by a morphological analyzer, so some forms slip through.

Search then runs in three passes and stops at the first one that returns something.

Which pass answered each of the 36 Korean sentence queries (both sets together), and how many of those put the answer in the top eight. In English 15 of 18, and in Japanese all 18, got their results in pass 1.

If no page reaches half the pieces in pass 3, pages holding any one piece are shown. Pages matching more pieces rank first, then by score. The score weighs a concept match most, then the title, tags, summary and body; a title that starts with the word earns 3 extra points and one that merely contains it earns 1. Remaining ties put evergreen pages ahead of dated news. That is why typing "harness" on the Korean screen puts the defining page, What a Harness Is, first.

Where search goes wrong

Korean verbs. Pass 2 has the lowest hit rate in the diagram (4 of 9). The dead piece it throws away is usually the verb that carries the question's meaning. Long conjugations, such as the Korean for "get loaded?" or "be trained?", do not shrink to the form the page uses, so they match nowhere. Once the verb is dropped, only common nouns remain, like "wiki" (41 pages), "connect" (41) and "claude" (59), and the answer sinks among them. For a Korean question asking when Claude Code loads skills, the right page came 19th.

Different words. The page that answers a Korean question about running Claude Code "on a company server" never uses the word for "company" and uses "self-hosting" six times. Every piece appears somewhere, so pass 1 returns results, but the answer is not among them. The same question in Japanese missed the same way. All 18 Japanese sentences got results in pass 1, yet 5 had the answer outside the top eight.

A neighbour that matches more pieces. For a question about how many stages Second Brain has, the per-level pages ranked above the introduction because they hold more of the query's pieces. The rule that favours pages matching more pieces worked backwards here.

How right the related block is

Edges in the related block are decided by the concepts two pages share. Inside one folder, two shared concepts connect them, or one rare concept that appears in three pages or fewer. Across folders it takes three shared concepts, or two where one is rare. A page with fewer than two concept neighbours is topped up with pages from its own folder, except in dated news folders. The full rule is in How Documents Connect.

The judging standard: "related" if someone who read the starting page would want the other next; "weak" if the topics overlap but it is not a natural next read; "unrelated" if one common concept or a homonym is all that joins them and following it goes nowhere. Two of the four unrelated edges are one pair, in both directions, between the Skills introduction and the security and roadmap page of the DOUZONE proposal; one of its two shared concepts, "reuse," is rare, so it clears the cross-folder bar. The other two are common-concept edges into the usage article. The other World-crossing pair is the "knowledge management" lineage between an LLM Wiki article and the DOUZONE knowledge platform proposal, which reflects a real relationship.

Recall is the weak side. Of the links authors wrote as "see also this," a third also sit in the related block at one hop, and only a seventh of those that cross folders. That is the price of a high bar across folders. The reflection on knowledge stores links to the Second Brain page on levels 3 to 5 in its text, but the two pages share only two concepts that are not rare ("RAG" and "second brain"), so the related block leaves it out. The bar sees what two pages share, not whether the link between them is good. Three pages that sit alone in their folders (the im-not-ai skill, the dews-sourcegraph skill and the notification engine proposal) have no neighbours to fill from, so their related block is empty.

On the tuning set, the related block often supplied the page search had missed, lifting 25% to 75%. On the frozen set, for the 2 questions where search found only one page, the related block did not hold the other, so one hop added nothing. There is a limit here too. Of the 20 pairs in both sets, 18 sit inside one folder, so these questions cannot say how much the related block helps with pairs across folders. Going by the recall figures above, that is probably where it is weak.

Why these numbers are not set beside other implementations'

LLM Wiki implementers have posted numbers too. One measured precision over 1,536 queries and reported 0.3086 on its fast tier; another reported 94.0% on the long-term memory benchmark LongMemEval against 71.2% for the comparison. This page does not set its figures beside theirs. They measured systems that store and recall memory on standard benchmarks; this page measured a published wiki's site search and related blocks on its own queries. The targets differ, and their figures are all self-reported. The aim here is not to make numbers look big but to publish the queries and judgments so anyone can measure the same way.

What to look at next

The numbers point two ways. In search: reducing Korean verbs to the forms pages use, and a synonym list or meaning-based search that connects "company server" to "self-hosting." In the graph: declaring as typed relations the good cross-folder links the bar misses. Either way, fresh queries get frozen before the change, and the same tool measures again.

Measurement conditions and fact-check note

Measured on 25 September 2026 against the wiki at commit 8907dbf: 71 of 72 indexed pages and 73 of 74 searchable pages, this page excluded. Search was run twice and gave identical numbers. The 48 frozen queries were fixed in a separate commit (ac66d57) before the current search engine was built; the 107 tuning queries were fixed before any results were seen, but their failures informed the engine's refinement. Both sets were written by someone who knows the pages, so scores may run higher than on real reader queries. In cells of 8 to 20 queries, one query moves the figure by 5 to 12.5 points. Judgments on unbacked edges were made by the AI that wrote this page, not by a person. The Claude Marketplace expansion (23 September, about 2,000 items, launched 6 March with six partners, purchases against existing commitments on a single invoice) was cross-checked with VentureBeat, The Next Web and the official page. The question on the LLM Wiki gist and the implementers' figures were confirmed in the gist's own comments; all figures are self-reported and no independent reproduction was found. The implementers are private individuals and are not named.
