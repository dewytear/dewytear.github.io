The Man Who Records Everything, and the Wiki That Publishes — Reflections on Knowledge Vaults

A reflection that reads the transcript of a conversation with a lifelogging practitioner, places his system side by side with this wiki, and draws out where this wiki should go next.

Where It Started — Three Questions

This piece began with three questions about this wiki itself. Is this wiki closer to RAG, to an LLM wiki, or can it be called a second brain? Three unfamiliar terms — so first, one line each.

  • RAG — every time you ask an AI a question, the system fetches relevant pieces from a pile of material on the spot and attaches them to the answer. It is a way of consuming knowledge, not the storehouse itself.
  • LLM wiki — a concept Karpathy proposed: a knowledge base that AI writes and organizes while a human curates. People and AI read the same documents.
  • Second brain — a second brain kept outside your head: a personal knowledge system that captures, organizes, connects, and retrieves.

The answers settled as follows. This wiki is not RAG but a canonical knowledge source that RAG can consume as-is (RAG-ready); its operation shares a skeleton with the LLM wiki, except that it is a public publication rather than a private vault; and on the five-level second-brain ladder it sits around level 3–4.

As an extension of those questions, I received the transcript of a conversation with a lifelogging practitioner who works the same subject at the opposite density. A necklace recorder captures his audio all day; a screen-recording tool captures his computer screen all day; everything flows into Obsidian (a Markdown note app), where he plans and reviews his days with AI — a self-described “recording maniac.” This piece dissects that conversation, compares it with this wiki, and lays out where this wiki goes next.

Dissecting the Conversation — His System Has Three Layers

① Capture layer — everything goes in, automatically. A necklace recorder (Omi) records audio all day; after two minutes of silence it closes an event, transcribes it, and files it into his notes. A screen-recording tool (Screenpipe) captures the screen around the clock while a small AI running on his own computer attaches a summary every 30 minutes. Meetings go through a dedicated recorder (PLAUD); web articles and videos land via a clipper, transcripts included; diaries are dictated by voice before bed. The point: he never has to decide whether to record — the willpower cost of capture is zero.

② Structure layer — each day is written in five strata. The daily note splits into intent (what I meant to do) → execution (records of what I actually did) → reality (what the screen recording caught that words never did) → interpretation (so how was it) → AI logs (records of work done with AI). Keeping plan and measurement apart makes review as precise as balancing a ledger. His storehouse splits in two — a raw folder holding originals untouched, and a wiki folder where refined notes live. The passage from raw to wiki is ingest: not a format conversion but pulling concepts out of material and linking them together. And the most refined move in the whole conversation — at ingest, the system asks back: “why did you capture this? where do you want to use it later?” The reason for keeping something is stored as data at the moment of keeping it; he calls this “making the unconscious conscious.”

③ Use layer — the records come back to their owner. At 2 a.m. every day, AI drafts a retrospective from the day's full data; weekly, monthly, quarterly, and yearly reviews stack on top. He once received a year-long narrative “exactly as it felt in his head,” and famously wove his dating-era records into a love story and used it to propose — a story that shows the emotional payoff of the system. His everyday entry point is a personal agent in a messenger; the next goal is an “advisory board” of mentor personas.

Two pipelines from the same lineage (Karpathy's LLM wiki) — the top treats a whole life, privately; the bottom treats knowledge, publicly.

Same Lineage, Different Choices — A Point-by-Point Comparison

The two systems speak a strikingly similar vocabulary: originals kept apart from refined notes (his raw/wiki, this wiki's documents/index), concepts extracted and linked at capture (his entities, this wiki's concepts & relations), logs of the work itself (his AI logs, this wiki's work log), and the maxim that “an ontology is a document, not a graph.” An ontology is the frame that decides what order your knowledge follows; he keeps his worldview and direction as documents and treats them as that order — and the exact same role here is played by the knowledge-system (kgs) document set. The difference is in what each optimizes.

Three Arguments the Conversation Raises

Argument 1 — the difference between RAG and an LLM wiki is when you tidy up. The conversation summarizes it in two points. RAG pays the cost of rebuilding its search index every time material grows, while an LLM wiki only needs to link the new documents in. And RAG's index (vectors) is unreadable to humans, while an LLM wiki stays ordinary readable documents, so people and AI share one canon. In this wiki's terms: RAG postpones the tidying until the moment a question arrives; an LLM wiki finishes the tidying at the moment of keeping. This wiki's entry pipeline (concept extraction, relation review, translation, index regeneration) is the extreme of the latter — which is exactly why knowledge-graph.json can be consumed by traversing a single file as-is. Still, the two are a division of layers, not rivals: semantic search (RAG-like) can sit on top of a well-tidied wiki, and that is in fact how this wiki's unified search works.

Argument 2 — the cause of failure is not the tool but the absence of purpose. The sharpest observation concerns why the second-brain boom mass-produced vaults that were built but never used — tombs of knowledge: people started without deciding what it was for, and had never structured themselves before. His remedy is the device above — at the moment of keeping, the system asks back “why did you keep this? where do you want to use it?” What is striking is that this wiki already encodes the same principle at a different layer: relations may only be declared when they actually exist in the text, forced links are forbidden, and concepts must appear in the body (the honesty rule). The moment collecting and connecting become the goal, the vault dies — he implements that insight as a question; this wiki implements it as gates and rules.

Argument 3 — an ontology is a document, not a graph. The conversation distinguishes the order (ontology) from the thing built in that order (the knowledge graph), and locates the order's substance in documents that say “who I am and how I think.” This wiki does exactly that — the World → Domain → System → Document hierarchy, the five-type relation vocabulary, and the rule that concepts are the keys joining documents all live as prose in the kgs documents, and the graph shown in Graph Studio is that order made visible. Keeping the rules in documents before code is also what lets an AI read the rules and follow them when it works on this repository.

Where This Wiki Goes Next

What this wiki should take from the comparison is not “record more.” Given its identity as a public publication, always-on capture of a whole life is not its path — and the conversation itself draws the line: “not everyone needs to do this; being able to retrieve when needed is enough.” Four things are worth taking.

  • Make the reason for keeping explicit — his “why did you keep this?” question can be grafted onto the entry procedure. Today a document's “why” is scattered across work logs; recording a one-line capture motive at entry time would improve later rediscovery.
  • Automate periodic retrospectives — work logs accumulate daily, but there is no monthly or quarterly review. Like his 2 a.m. retrospective, a scheduled job that drafts a monthly review from work logs, commits, and the backlog is the cheapest first step toward level 5 (an always-on brain OS).
  • A lightweight inbox layer — like his raw/wiki split, this wiki has no layer for “material before the value judgment.” The entry bar (three languages, relations, gates) is high enough that good material can be lost; a private, non-published inbox beside the wiki, promoting only what matures, is worth considering.
  • A conversational entry point — today AI consumption is left to whoever reads the exports. A QA entry point that knows this wiki, like his personal agent, would extend consumption into dialogue while leaving the canon untouched.

In one line — he is building a system that records a life and gives it back to its owner; this wiki is building a system that refines knowledge and publishes it to everyone, human and AI. Two extremes of one lineage, with clear things to learn from each other: from him, motive-as-data and automated retrospectives; from this wiki, what he might take is machine discipline and the sustainability of public publication. A knowledge vault succeeds or fails not by tools or volume, but by whether purpose is engraved into the structure — that is the conclusion of this comparison.
