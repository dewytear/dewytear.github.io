The One Who Deleted and the One Who Rendered — Cherny's “80% of the Harness” and Karpathy's “LOTR Movie”

In the two weeks after Opus 5 shipped, the two most-shared scenes in the AI world looked like opposites. Boris Cherny — creator of Claude Code — said in a talk that when the new model arrived, his team deleted more than 80% of the system prompt, and the model got smarter. Andrej Karpathy declared we are “leaving the territory” of testing LLMs with SVG pelicans, handed Opus 5 the first paragraph of The Lord of the Rings and a $10 budget, and got back a 3D animation that runs in the browser. One story is about erasing, the other about conjuring — and they are two faces of the same event. This article cross-verifies both against multiple reports, lays out the details, and analyzes why they are one story.

Three-line summary

  • When Opus 5 arrived, Cherny's team deleted more than 80% of Claude Code's system prompt — and the model ran “slightly smarter”. The methodology is ablation (delete everything, reintroduce line by line, measure); the advice: “sabotage yourselves — delete your system prompt every six months”.
  • Karpathy gave Opus 5 the first paragraph of LOTR plus a 1M-token budget (~$10) and asked for a three.js render — about two hours later it had written 5,500 lines of code that procedurally render a 3D movie, playable and forkable in the browser (embedded below).
  • The shared structure: as models improve, scaffolding gets deleted, output scale jumps, and the bottleneck moves to verification and perception — the weakness Karpathy flagged (“LLMs can't watch their own video; they audit by slowly taking screenshots”) is exactly that bottleneck.

Cherny — what “we deleted 80% and it got smarter” actually means

Cherny created Claude Code and now heads it — so this is the harness's own author deleting the harness. Piecing the reports together:

  • What was deleted — more than 80% of Claude Code's system prompt when Opus 5 landed. Behaviors that older models had to be told about, the new model already does without being told.
  • The result — not degradation but improvement: in Cherny's words the model became “slightly smarter”. Stale scaffolding is not neutral — it is a cost that fights the model. Instructions written to patch an old model's weaknesses become shackles once those weaknesses are gone.
  • The methodology: ablation — this was not a hunch. The team deletes the entire system prompt and reintroduces it line by line, measuring the impact of each line, for every model generation. When a new model arrives, the default posture is “throw essentially everything away and start over”.
  • The advice — generalized for everyone: “sabotage yourselves — delete your system prompt every six months”. A harness is not an asset; it is a consumable that depreciates.
  • The next layer: loops — from the same talk lineage comes the line “I don't prompt Claude anymore — I have loops that prompt Claude”. The human job moves up from writing one good prompt to designing the loop in which the model observes, plans, acts and reflects for hours or days. The showcase: thousands of parallel agents rewriting the Bun runtime from Zig to Rust in 11 days.

Read through this wiki's harness engineering series — which defines the harness as “the whole system in which the AI works” — Cherny's remarks are not the abolition of the harness but its generational turnover. What got deleted is the prompt layer, the fastest to go stale; the upper layers — loops, verification, tools — got thicker. The precise reading is not “harnesses matter less” but “the harness's center of gravity moved from instructions to structure”.

Karpathy — leaving the pelican for Middle-earth

There is a lineage here. For years, the community's informal test for every new model was “draw an SVG of a pelican riding a bicycle” — making the model draw with code to probe its spatial and structural sense. In this post Karpathy declared “we're starting to leave that territory”, and showed the generalized version himself.

  • Input: the first paragraph of The Lord of the Rings + a 1M-token budget (~$10) + a request to render it in three.js. That's all — no storyboard, no assets, no scene list.
  • Output: about two hours later, 5,500 lines of code — placing and orchestrating polygon assets at (x,y,z) coordinates, writing the camera work and animation procedurally: a 3D movie that runs in the browser.
  • Karpathy's verdict: “kind of janky but fun” — and “it's a bit mindboggling” that the LLM had to place and choreograph all of it.

What matters just as much is the weakness Karpathy flagged: moving artifacts — games, worlds — are hard for an LLM to audit. Models cannot natively perceive video, so Opus 5 had to slowly, painstakingly take screenshots at different points to check its own scene, and it slipped a few times along the way — hence the jank. It can build the thing but cannot watch what it built — the most precise one-liner this experiment leaves behind.

Why this is one story

Put the two scenes side by side and the same structure appears.

Cherny (left) and Karpathy (center) are two faces of the same event — and both point at the box on the right.
  • Instructions get deleted. As models internalize behaviors, the “do it this way” layer depreciates — Cherny's 80% is the measured value of that depreciation.
  • Making gets cheap. In a world where one paragraph yields a 3D movie, the marginal cost of an artifact has fallen to $10 and two hours — Karpathy's experiment is the specimen.
  • So verification becomes the bottleneck. Karpathy's model audited itself by screenshots because it can't watch its own video; Cherny's answer is to have loops, not humans, run the verification. And last week OpenAI showing up with Lean machine-checked certificates for its mathematics is a different answer at the same spot — as generation becomes abundant, verification becomes precious.

This meets the conclusion this wiki reached in “The Harness Is Not Enough” — a harness's value lies not in the thickness of its instructions but in the quality of its verification loop. Cherny testified to that thesis as the harness's own author; Karpathy testified to it with the scale of the artifact.
