LLM Wiki and Knowledge Context — Team Knowledge Compiled at Write Time

This document starts from Andrej Karpathy's LLM Wiki proposal, then covers the “Knowledge Context” implementation (an Obsidian vault + a Git repository) that one team actually runs at team scale, the index explosion problem they hit as it grew and their adoption of OpenViking, and finally the governance that split the roles of humans and agents. Rather than simply relaying the case, we checked the original sources of the tools and proposals it mentioned (Karpathy's gist, GitHub repositories, official docs) on the web to back up the claims, and laid out the core processes as diagrams.

① The problem — the knowledge exists, but you explain it again every time

The starting point is a familiar scene. A team's knowledge is scattered across chat logs, documents, and personal notes, so every time a new member joins or an agent is given a task, you explain the same context from scratch again. Shared documents multiply into “final” and “really_final” copies until nobody knows which one is current (Google Docs-style copy proliferation), and the most valuable thing of all — tacit knowledge about why a decision was made and which attempts failed — is never recorded and stays only inside people's heads. In the LLM era this cost is also billed in tokens: every time you call an agent, you pour the same background explanation into the context all over again.

② RAG and LLM Wiki — retrieval at read time vs. compilation at write time

The usual answer to this problem is RAG (retrieval-augmented generation). You split documents into pieces (chunks), embed them, and the moment a question arrives you find similar pieces and slot them into the context — in other words, you defer organizing knowledge to read (query) time. The LLM Wiki that Karpathy proposed flips the direction. If an LLM reads, organizes, and consolidates material at the moment it comes in, turning it into wiki documents that humans can read, then at query time you can just read that document with no retrieval pipeline. Karpathy compares this to a compiler — the original material is source code, the LLM is the compiler, and the wiki is the build output; if RAG is an interpreter that digs through the source on every run, LLM Wiki is the side that compiles ahead of time.

Same knowledge, different moment — RAG looks things up on every query, LLM Wiki organizes them once, on the way in.
RAG (retrieval at read time)LLM Wiki (compilation at write time)
When organizing happensAt query time (ad hoc)When material arrives (once, up front)
Query costRetrieval pipeline + chunk injection, repeatedReading a document (no pipeline needed)
Shape of knowledgeChunks — hard for humans to readConsolidated documents — shared by humans and LLMs
ConsistencyContradictions between chunks are hard to detectConsolidated and checked in the compile and lint stages
Where it fitsVast, rarely used material where quoting the original mattersLiving knowledge the team uses over and over

The two are not mutually exclusive — this case, too, assumes they run side by side: “compile the core knowledge you use often into the wiki, and leave the vast original texts to retrieval.”

③ Karpathy's LLM Wiki — a three-layer structure with index.md and log.md

The skeleton of the proposal Karpathy published as a gist is a three-layer structure. At the bottom sit the original sources (incoming material kept verbatim and immutable); above them is the wiki layer, which the LLM maintains as concept-level markdown by reading the originals (humans are read-only — if you want a change, you put material into the source layer and have it recompiled); and on top of it all is the schema layer (CLAUDE.md / AGENTS.md-style conventions: document format, naming, and linking rules). Two management files are attached to this — index.md, a table of contents of every entry, and log.md, which is only ever appended to in the form ## [date] action | target. The operations boil down to three: ingest (compile new material), query (answer questions), and lint (check the whole wiki for contradictions and broken links).

The three layers: sources stay immutable, the LLM manages the wiki, the schema enforces the conventions — with index.md and log.md carrying the table of contents and the history.
How this wiki connects The wiki you are reading right now is exactly a variant of this structure — the body text (HTML fragments) is the wiki layer, list and the knowledge index play the role of index.md, the Work Log plays the role of log.md, and the CLAUDE.md rules plus the validate gates play the role of schema and lint. ④ Knowledge Context — transplanted to a team with an Obsidian vault + Git

The heart of it is a real implementation that moves Karpathy's personal proposal to team scale. A single repository is the team's knowledge context, and physically it is both an Obsidian vault and a Git repository — non-developers write notes in the Obsidian UI, and developers send PRs through Git. They are just two doors onto the same files. The folder structure is also the permission structure:

  • context/ — the team shared layer. The team's official context, which only knowledge that has passed verification enters.
  • members/<name>/ — the personal working layer. Each person's drafts, experiments, and notes. Anything goes here.
  • agents/ — agent settings and skills. It exists in both the shared layer and the personal layer, so a skill someone builds gets promoted to a team skill after verification. AGENTS.md serves as the team's official system prompt.
  • _private/ — an area excluded from sync. There is no promotion path at all.

The core process is the promotion flow by which personal knowledge becomes team knowledge. To lift a note from the personal layer into the shared layer you open a PR, which goes through automated verification (format, links, frontmatter) and an AI review (duplication, convention violations, conflicts with existing documents), and only after a human approves it at the end is it merged into context/. Frontmatter (metadata) is enforced at input time by Obsidian Templates, and Git history automatically keeps a record of everything.

It is not only knowledge — skills pass through the same gate, the path by which a good personal skill becomes a team asset.

The team counts four effects. (1) Instead of background explanations, a single note link conveys the context; (2) you stop pouring the same explanation into agents over and over, which saves tokens; (3) personal skills and prompts ride the promotion flow and spread across the whole team; and (4) because the official context is a single repository, the “final_really_final” copy problem disappears structurally. Tacit knowledge such as the background to a decision also accumulates in PR descriptions and notes.

⑤ The wall of scale — markdown explosion and OpenViking

Once operations settled in, a different problem arrived. As notes grew into the thousands, the index files swelled to 20–30 files of 1,000–3,000 lines each, and an agent reading nothing but the indexes to get its bearings already burned a sizable share of the context — the tokens saved by compiling at write time were being eaten back by the cost of finding things. The solution the team adopted is OpenViking, released by ByteDance: a “context database” that unifies an agent's memory, knowledge (RAG), and skills into a single filesystem paradigm, in which every piece of knowledge has a viking:// path. The key is layered loading — every node carries three levels of summary, L0 (one-line summary), L1 (overview), and L2 (full text), and the agent skims everything cheaply with L0, plans using the L1 of the candidates, and only pulls down L2 for the documents it really needs. Recursive directory-level search (narrowing the location with a folder's L0 and L1, then drilling down into it) replaces the giant indexes.

Do not read it all — climb down the ladder of summaries: progressive L0 → L1 → L2 loading replaces the index explosion.

The team also built a mapping of existing folders onto OpenViking's types — reference material to resources, the rationale behind decisions and meeting notes to evidence, agent skills to skills, conversation and work memory to memory, with anything not yet classified left as unindexed and folded in gradually. Another reason cited for adopting it was observability: you can see which paths search and loading read, and how much of them.

⑥ Governance — what humans hold on to, what is handed to agents

“Agents manage the wiki” does not mean “hand them everything.” The team's principle is an inverted whitelist: agents by default, with humans specifying the exceptions:

WhoResponsible forWhy
Humans onlythe _private/ areaThere is no sync or promotion path at all — no reason for a machine to reach in
DeletionRemoving knowledge is hard to undo and requires judgment about context
Sensitive permissions, final approvalResponsibility for merges into the shared layer rests with people
AgentsEverything elseMachines are more diligent at repetitive work — organizing, consolidating, linking, summarizing, reviewing

Three mechanisms hold this up — frontmatter is enforced at input time by Obsidian Templates, which cuts down after-the-fact corrections; Git history keeps a complete record of who (human or agent) changed what and when; and the human approval gate in the promotion flow guards the quality floor of the shared layer. It is the same philosophy as the “leave the development flow alone, let AI turn it into knowledge” stance taken by this wiki's Douzone knowledge platform proposal.

⑦ The tool ecosystem — agents have started handling Obsidian

The last signal worth noting is on the tooling side. Obsidian CEO Steph Ango (kepano) has released obsidian-skills, five SKILL.md files in the Agent Skills format that teach coding agents like Claude Code to correctly read and write Obsidian's file formats (wikilinks and callouts in .md, .base databases, .canvas canvases). Obsidian has also started shipping a CLI, which means the arrangement described in this article — “humans through the Obsidian UI, agents through files, the CLI, and skills” on the same vault — is now backed by official tooling. For teams that, like Knowledge Context, have chosen markdown + Git, it means the agent-side door is being standardized.

In summary — in three sentences

  • Move organizing knowledge from query time (RAG) to write time (wiki compilation) and you get a single context that the team and the agents both read.
  • At team scale, structure is governance — separating the personal, shared, and private layers plus a promotion PR flow is what creates quality and accountability.
  • As knowledge grows, the cost of finding becomes the bottleneck — layered summaries (L0, L1, L2) and recursive drill-down are the next step beyond giant indexes.
