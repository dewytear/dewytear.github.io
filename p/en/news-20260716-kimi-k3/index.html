Kimi K3 — a 2.8-trillion-parameter open-weight model reaches the frontier

On July 16, 2026, the Chinese AI company Moonshot AI unveiled its new AI model Kimi K3. In one line: “for the first time, an open model anyone can download has come right up under the chin of the world’s top paid models.” Every figure printed here was cross-checked against multiple independent sources via web search, and anything corrected or filtered out during that check is collected separately in the “fact-check notes” at the end.

Three-line summary

  • Kimi K3 is the largest open (open-weight) model ever — a brain size (parameter count) of 2.8 trillion.
  • On several public tests (benchmarks) it sits third, just below Claude Fable 5 and GPT-5.6, and in some areas such as building web screens it is first.
  • Yet its price is a tier cheaper than the top-ranked models. Because it broke the received wisdom that “open models trail American paid models by six months to a year,” the industry was thrown into a stir.

Five terms to learn first

Know just these five and everything below reads.

TermPlain explanation
ParameterThe number of adjustment screws inside a model. During training these screws turn little by little, and knowledge and ability get engraved into them. The more there are, the more the model can hold — at the cost of higher expense to build and run.
TokenThe smallest piece of text as an AI reads and writes. Roughly one word is 1–2 tokens. AI usage fees are also billed by token count.
Context windowHow much a model can hold in mind and work on at once. K3 has 1 million tokens — big enough to hand it several thick books whole and say “read all of this and answer.”
Open weightsReleasing the finished model’s ‘brain file’ (the weights) in its entirety. Anyone can download it, run it on their own server, and modify it. The opposite is closed — the OpenAI/Anthropic approach of hiding the file and selling only access.
BenchmarkA report card from giving many models the same exam and grading it. There are many kinds — coding tests, general-knowledge tests, real-work tests — and every ranking in this article is a benchmark ranking.

What happened

  • Launch: 2026-07-16, Moonshot AI (Beijing). Usable right away through the app and the API (the paid access channel for developers).
  • Size: 2.8 trillion parameters — the largest in the history of open models.
  • Memory: a 1 million token context window, multimodality that also understands drawings and photos, and a three-level reasoning mode that dials thinking deeper or shallower.
  • Caution: although announced as “open weights,” the brain file itself is scheduled for release on 2026-07-27. As of this writing it is still usable only through the app and API.

Secret ① — Calling only 16 of 896 experts (MoE)

“Wouldn’t running 2.8 trillion parameters cost a fortune?” — it would. That is why K3 uses a structure called MoE (Mixture of Experts). Picture a large general hospital. There are 896 specialists, but not all 896 examine a single patient. The front desk looks at the symptoms and calls in only the 16 who are actually needed.

K3 works exactly like that. Every time one piece of text (a token) comes in, the router (the front desk) picks only 16 of the 896 expert neural networks and puts them to work. So while the whole knowledge warehouse is a huge 2.8 trillion, the part actually working at any moment is only about 50 billion (50B) — an elephant’s knowledge with a cheetah’s stride.

In hospital terms: the front desk (router) calls in only 16 of 896 specialists. The knowledge warehouse is 2.8 trillion, but only ~50B works at any moment.

Secret ② — Reads long, and does not forget as it deepens (KDA · AttnRes)

These are the other two improvements Moonshot described on its official blog. The names are hard, but what they do is simple.

  • Kimi Delta Attention (KDA) — while an AI reads text it keeps computing “which word should I focus on” (this is called attention), and the longer the text, the more this computation snowballs. KDA is a way of making that computation lighter, raising the speed of writing answers over long 1-million-token documents by up to 6.3×. It is like reading with a well-organized summary memo in hand instead of underlining the whole book.
  • Attention Residuals (AttnRes) — an AI model refines its thinking as it passes through dozens to hundreds of layers, and as the layers get deeper there is a problem: important information seen earlier gradually fades. AttnRes is a device that picks out and pulls back information from earlier layers when it is needed. It is similar to re-checking the original note partway through a game of telephone so the message does not get distorted.

Thanks to these structural improvements, Moonshot explains, K3’s ‘fuel efficiency’ improved about 2.5× over its predecessor K2. That does not mean “2.5× smarter”; it means it extracts 2.5× more ability from the same amount of compute and training data.

In the official diagram Moonshot published, three things fit into one picture — Stable LatentMoE, which selects and uses experts, KDA, which reads long text lightly, and AttnRes, which pulls important information back across the blocks (the horizontal lines on the right).

The report card — how good is it

Overall score: right under the top spot

On the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index (an average of 9 tests, a sort of ‘mock-exam total’) that adds many test results into one, K3 scored 57.1. That is third, behind first-place Claude Fable 5 Max (59.9) and second-place GPT-5.6 Sol Max (58.9). The score gap is under 3 points — as the figure below shows, they are essentially shoulder to shoulder.

The two above are closed and paid, K3 is an open (soon-to-be-released) model — this narrow gap is the heart of this launch.

First place chosen by people themselves: building web screens

LMArena is an unusual testing ground. It poses a question, shows two models’ answers side by side with the names hidden, and has people vote for the better one. Those wins and losses pile up as a score (Elo), like a chess ranking. In Frontend Code Arena, where models build web screens (frontends), K3 took first place with 1,679 points — beating Claude Fable 5. Since its predecessor K2.6 was 18th, it jumped 17 places in a single generation, and it was first in 6 of the 7 subcategories (only in the games category was it second, behind Fable 5). When they faced off on the same question, the rate at which K3’s answer was chosen (its win rate) averaged 76% — meaning it won 76 out of 100 matchups.

It is strong at writing too. On one writing leaderboard that blind-grades real scripts (run by Louie), it took first place with 2,840 Elo, the first time an open model has taken the top spot on that board (its predecessor was 21st). On the Vals Index, which evaluates mainly practical work tasks, it was second (74.7%) out of 38 models.

Hold on — you should not take self-measured scores at face value

Same model, same test, a 7-point difference The Terminal-Bench score of 88.3 in the table above is a number Moonshot measured itself. Yet when the evaluation firm Vals re-measured it independently, it came out around 80.9. Rather than trickery, this is because what tools the model was handed and what settings it was tested under — that is, the ‘harness’ (the execution environment wrapped around the model) — differed. It is like the same driver posting a different lap time in a different car. The lesson: an AI report card must always be read together with “who measured it, under what conditions.”

Price — why everyone is talking about “value for money”

An AI model’s API is metered by the number of tokens you pay for. The question (input) and the answer (output) are each priced.

ItemPrice (per million tokens)
Input$3.00
Input — cache hit$0.30 (an automatic 90% discount when the same content is sent repeatedly)
Output$15.00

What this price means only becomes visible through comparison. As the industry sums it up, “Opus 4.8-class performance at Sonnet 5 prices” — that is, it sells a top-tier model’s ability at the price of the tier below (Latent.space). On top of that, there is no surcharge even when you feed in a 1-million-token document. There is an opposite reading too: since it costs far more than its predecessor K2, some read it as a signal that “the era of ultra-cheap Chinese AI is over” (The Decoder). Both are two sides of the same fact — it is cheaper than the top tier and more expensive than the Chinese open models of the past.

What can you build with it — the official demos

Moonshot’s showcase focuses less on “jobs that finish in one go” and more on long-horizon autonomous work — the mode where a person hands over a task and walks away while the model plans, builds, checks results, and fixes on its own for hours or days.

  • Semiconductor chip design — running alone for 24–48 hours, it designed and even verified a small AI-dedicated chip with open design tools (4mm² of area, processing 8,700 tokens per second in simulation). In effect, an AI designed “a chip to run AI on.”
  • Scientific data analysis — it analyzed 391 gravitational-wave observation events (ripples in spacetime made by things like black hole collisions) by commanding more than 20 subagents (helper AI workers the main model directs) at once, and produced 7 kinds of visualizations plus summaries of more than 10 papers.
  • Games and 3D — a wuxia open-world RPG, a colosseum, a fighting game, a Game Boy emulator, a 3D typewriter that disassembles into parts, a black hole simulation and more, all built for the browser. Notably it uses a feedback loop — the same one human developers use — of taking screenshots of the screens it built, checking them with its own eyes, and fixing them.
  • Motion graphics and video — because one model handles text, images, and video together, it also produced things like an animated video explaining its own architecture.

Kernel optimization — speeding up the code that makes up its own body

What developers paid the most attention to in the showcase was GPU kernel optimization. A kernel is a small, core computation program that runs on the GPU, and an AI model’s speed is ultimately decided by how fast these kernels are. The experiment was designed like this — each model was put in the same sandbox and made to profile, rewrite, and benchmark 4 kinds of kernel tasks (AttnRes, DSA, 512-head MLA, KDA) on its own for up to 24 hours (on an NVIDIA H200 and a third-party GPGPU). The result: K3 was first in 3 of the 4 tasks, with Claude Fable 5 first only on DSA. On the AttnRes task in particular, one forward + backward pass fell from 283.6ms to 114.4ms — in effect, it optimized the code that makes up its own architecture (AttnRes, KDA).

How to read this All of the cases above are Moonshot’s own promotional showcase. The outputs are indeed public, but they are not results reproduced or verified by a third party, so it is more accurate to read them as “work in this direction (long-running autonomous tasks) has become possible to assign.”

How the world reacted

  • “The era of catching up is over” — OpenAI researcher roon commented, “the era in which Chinese labs were far behind is over. Kimi is at least on par with today’s leading open models.” He added the caveat, though, that “some people who actually use it will find it less useful than today’s report card suggests.”
  • Safety worries — with a closed model the company can cut off access if something goes wrong, but once open weights are out, they cannot be recalled. So “should a model this strong be released at all, and in particular how good is it at hacking (cyberattacks)?” instantly became a point of contention. Along with the observation that the cyber capability test (CyberGym) score is missing from the released report card, voices called for independent evaluation by bodies such as the UK AI Safety Institute (AISI), and there was also a counterargument that “excessive fear is unnecessary” (Transformer).
  • Even the stock market wobbled — reports followed that semiconductor and AI-related shares, which stood on the outlook that “money will pour into expensive paid models and giant infrastructure,” plunged right after the launch. It is being called a replay of the ‘DeepSeek shock,’ when the cheap Chinese model DeepSeek shook the market in early 2025.

Fact-check notes — what was corrected or excluded

Claims spread in early reviews and the community that were corrected or excluded · “3 trillion parameters” → the figure on which official and press sources agree is 2.8 trillion (2.8T) (reflected in the body)
· “the weights are already public on the web” → release is scheduled (2026-07-27); at the time of writing, API only
· “GDPval is a benchmark OpenAI built” → what this article cites is the variant operated by Artificial Analysis (GDPval-AA v2)
· Excluded (failed cross-check): one user’s “CSGO clone for $324” case, the “$0.94 per unit of intelligence vs Opus $1.80” cost metric, the mention of Meta’s “Muse Spark,” the 58% win-rate figure for competing models, and individual tweets about debugging failures — these were not confirmed by multiple independent sources, so they are not included.
