AI 2040: Plan A — Policy and Verification (the Mechanisms That Hold the Deal Together)

This installment follows the AI 2040: Plan A overview and lays out the policy mechanisms that keep the scenario rolling. In Plan A's own words, the axis is a “verified deal” — everyone slows down, and everyone watches everyone else. What follows has been cross-checked against the public summary and the reporting on the Supplements.

① Total Research Transparency

The core norm is to publish all frontier AI research so that anyone can see it. The effects break down into three.

  • Removes the incentive to hide breakthroughs — there is no longer any gain in secretly pulling ahead.
  • Mutual auditing — rival labs can audit each other's safety work.
  • Blocks covert alignment — secretly instilling a particular loyalty in a model becomes all but impossible.

② Verification protocol — network taps + partial recomputation

This is the method for technically confirming a claim of “being transparent.” Much like inference verification:

  • Network taps collect evidence of a data center's workloads, and
  • partial recomputation spot-checks whether the packets making up that workload actually match the declared computation.

In other words, when the other side says “we only computed within the bounds of the deal,” this is a structure that confirms the claim by computation instead of trusting it.

③ Relocating data centers — indefensible locations

The most striking mechanism. R&D data centers are deliberately placed in locations their own country would find hard to defend, making it hard to defect from the deal.

United StatesMongolia
ChinaCanada

On top of this, SL5-level security for model weights and integrity of verification is applied. The original model offers a concrete numerical trajectory — for instance, the data centers reciprocally built in Canada and Mongolia running at gigawatt scale at a given point (Canada ~1GW, Mongolia ~2GW) and reaching a certain share (about 15%) of the world's compute by year-end.

④ Mutually Assured Compute Destruction

This transplants the Cold War logic of nuclear deterrence (MAD) onto data centers. If the deal breaks down and the other side tries to seize a cluster, the side guarding that cluster destroys its own compute with a “scorched-earth” mechanism. The United States places such a mechanism at the Mongolia cluster and China at the Canada cluster, symmetrically. “Even if you take it, you can't use it” is what drives the gain from defecting to zero.

⑤ Model spec · third-party oversight

Alongside the mechanisms above, the model's goals and behavioral norms are codified in a public model spec, and compliance with the deal is overseen by third parties. Even critics single out these three (research transparency, the model spec, and third-party oversight) as the strongest part of Plan A (see Reactions and critiques).

The original's Supplements The details of the verification plan and the economic model are provided as an interactive explorer in the original's Supplements (modeling the economy through capital, robots, prices, land, and energy, plus the verification plan in detail). This wiki carries only a static summary and leaves reproducing the simulator out of scope.
Facts and estimates The design intent of these mechanisms (transparency, verification, relocation, compute destruction) is confirmed by public materials. Whether they could actually work, however — the political realism of the US and China agreeing to place their nations' core compute in indefensible locations, and the completeness of verification — is heavily contested.
