Why Now — Development Knowledge That Scatters and Disappears

Running a project keeps producing knowledge: Architecture and design structure, business Rules and state transitions, the meaning of DB Tables and Columns, API integrations, causes of incidents and how they were fixed, performance improvement cases, reusable development patterns. Yet most of this knowledge stays scattered across Source Code · Commits · Merge Requests · Issues · messengers · email · meetings · personal memory, and never accumulates as organizational knowledge.

The Cost Has Already Been Quantified

  • 62% of developers spend more than 30 minutes a day looking for answers, and 68% run into knowledge silos at least once a week — Stack Overflow Developer Survey 2022
  • 69% of developers lose more than 8 hours a week (about 20% of working hours) to inefficiency, and point to "insufficient documentation" as a leading cause — Atlassian, State of Developer Experience 2024
  • Knowledge workers spend 19% of their working hours searching for and gathering information — McKinsey, The Social Economy (2012)
  • 42% of job knowledge is unique knowledge held only by that one person, so when they leave, a colleague cannot perform 42% of that work. For large enterprises, the loss from inefficient knowledge sharing is estimated at $47 million per year — Panopto Workplace Knowledge Report (2018)
  • It takes a new developer an average of 3 to 9 months to reach full productivity — DX, Developer Onboarding Research

As a result, organizations keep repeating the same things: re-analyzing the same problem, losing design intent and the background of changes, failing to reuse experience across projects, losing knowledge when people move or leave, paying a high onboarding cost for new developers, and re-explaining Context over and over when using AI development tools.

"Let's Just Write Better Documents" Is Not the Answer

In an open source survey, 93% named "incomplete or outdated documentation" as a pervasive problem while 60% rarely contributed to documentation at all (GitHub Open Source Survey 2017), and Stack Overflow analyzed early on the structural reasons why internal wikis and documentation databases get abandoned and lose trust (Why Your Wikis … Don't Work, 2019). On development floors where schedules and priorities always win, documentation that depends on human diligence does not last.

Redefining the problem What is missing is not developer diligence but structure. The knowledge is already piling up in GitLab — it just does not have to be a human who reads and organizes it. And half of that structure (the engine that holds, links, and verifies knowledge) is already in operation as the Reference Wiki — the only missing piece is the connection to GitLab. The next installment, The Core of the Proposal, covers that structure.
