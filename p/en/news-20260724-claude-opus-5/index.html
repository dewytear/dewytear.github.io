Claude Opus 5 — A Near-Fable at Half the Price Has Arrived

On July 24, 2026, Anthropic unveiled Claude Opus 5. In one line — “performance right up against the top-tier Fable 5, at half the price.” This announcement came amid a sprint of four model releases in two months, and the reason reviewers were buzzing is simple: on several benchmarks the numbers came out beating Anthropic’s own top-tier model. This article is built on the facts cross-verified against the official announcement and multiple press reports, together with what came up in the review reactions right after launch — verified facts and unconfirmed reactions are marked separately.

Three-line summary

  • Opus 5 is $5/$25 (input/output, per million tokens) — the same price as its predecessor Opus 4.8, yet the performance is right on the heels of Fable 5 ($10/$50), or above it on some benchmarks.
  • The real news is not only the duel with Fable — the generational jump from Opus 4.8 to 5 is large across every category, and in particular long-horizon agentic work that “verifies itself and sees things through” got noticeably better (symbolized by the demo where, unable to view a drawing, it wrote a CV pipeline itself and rebuilt the part as 3D CAD).
  • The practical concern is safeguards — since early July there has been controversy over Fable 5’s safety classifier false-positiving even on security and debugging work, and the point reviewers are watching is that Opus 5 is far looser about that constraint.

Just three terms to know going in

TermPlain explanation
effort
(thinking intensity)		The dial that controls how long the model thinks about a single answer. The higher you push it — high → xhigh → max — the higher the score, but the more tokens (= money) it spends. It is the key axis of this comparison.
token efficiencyHow few tokens it takes to produce the same result. Even a cheap unit price gets expensive if the model is long-winded — Opus 5 has both half the unit price and a tendency to use fewer tokens, so the ‘cost per task’ drops sharply.
long-horizon agentic workWork that is not answered once and done, but completed by repeating attempt → verification → revision dozens of times once a goal is given. Real-world tasks like coding agents and computer use all belong here.

Benchmarks — “Fable only wins at max”

This is the part the post-launch reviews were most excited about. On the practical coding benchmark CursorBench 3.2, Fable 5 leads only narrowly in the max range where effort is pushed all the way up (within 0.5% of the peak), and in the high and xhigh ranges that most users actually use, the output is effectively the same — yet the cost per task is half.

A conceptual chart tracing the composition of the official one (not to measured scale). That the two curves almost lie on top of each other — that is the one picture of this announcement.

The commentary in the review reactions lands on this same point — that “more than the Fable-versus-Opus framing, the jump from 4.8 to 5 is the real news.” Every category rose substantially over the predecessor, and Fable 5 kept its edge only in a few areas such as multidisciplinary reasoning (without tools), law and medicine (and even there it flips once tools are used, is the reviewers’ observation).

The highlight — “If it cannot see, it builds a way to see”

This is the case from the announcement that gets talked about most. Opus 5 was given a single mechanical part drawing and told to “write code that rebuilds this as a 3D CAD model” — but it was deliberately given no means of opening and viewing the drawing. A situation with a goal but the method blocked. Instead of giving up, Opus 5 wrote a computer vision pipeline itself to extract the shape geometry from the image pixels, and with it rebuilt the whole part as a FreeCAD 3D model. Competing models under the same conditions failed on all five attempts.

“Give it only the goal (the north star) and it finds a method and sees it through” — the emblematic case of the long-horizon agentic ability said to have improved most over Opus 4.8.

Why does this matter? Most of what we ask AI to do in practice is not one-shot but a loop — give a goal, verify the result, have it fix things again. This is exactly the point Anthropic stressed when it said the model “has become stronger at verifying its work and iterating carefully,” and the case above is a demonstration that this ability extends as far as “inventing the method.”

Safeguards — the context of the Fable 5 false-positive controversy

This is the part the review reactions picked as “realistically the most welcome change.” There is a backstory — after Fable 5’s safety classifier was strengthened in early July, the problem of it false-positiving even security code review or ordinary debugging as dangerous requests and downgrading them to a lower model (Opus 4.8) became a public issue (numerous GitHub issues, reports of debugging benchmarks plunging). Opus 5 uses the same classifier system but has been tuned so it trips far less sensitively — that is the common observation of the announcement and the reviews. Some reviews mentioned a figure of “an 85% reduction in trigger probability versus Fable,” but that specific number could not be confirmed in official documents, so it is flagged separately in the fact-check notes below.

One more thing — that problem-behavior scores in alignment evaluations are lower than in the predecessors (lower is better), and that the ability to find vulnerabilities and exploits in open source code has risen substantially, are also observations from the review reactions. For detailed numbers, the official system card (PDF) is the primary source.

On top of the unit price being half, token efficiency (the number of tokens spent on the same job) is also on the good side, so the ‘cost per task’ gap the benchmarks showed widens further than the unit-price difference (clearing Fable’s peak on ⅓ the budget in OSWorld is an example). It is usable on Claude.ai, the API, Claude Code and Cowork right at launch, and it has become the default model of the Claude Max plan.

Fact-check notes

  • Passed cross-verification (numbers in the body) — the launch date (7/24), pricing ($5/$25, Fable $10/$50), CursorBench 3.2 (within 0.5% at max, half the cost per task), Frontier-Bench 43.3% vs 34.4%, OSWorld ⅓ budget, AutomationBench ~1.5×, ARC-AGI 3 3×, the three demos, and the CAD case (competing models losing all five attempts) match between the official announcement and multiple independent reports.
  • Claims from review reactions (official numbers unconfirmed) — ① the “85% reduction” in safeguard triggers: the direction (loosening) is confirmed by the official announcement and by reporting, but the figure of 85% could not be confirmed. ② “Sonnet 5 is the most expensive model per task”: a reviewer’s calculation claim, with no independent verification. ③ Alignment evaluation and vulnerability-finding numbers: the system card is the primary source, and the body describes only the direction.
  • The diagrams are conceptual — the CursorBench curves and the demo panels trace the composition of the official chart and the public demos, and are not to measured scale (the numbers follow the tables).
  • How to read benchmark numbers — even the same model scores very differently depending on which harness (tools, context, retry settings) it is run on. A case where self-announced numbers and independently reproduced numbers diverge was actually confirmed in the Kimi K3 article — the table above, too, is safer read as ‘the shape of performance per cost’ than as a ‘ranking’.
