How Companies Adopt Ontologies — Cases · ROI · The Four-Step Build

Where the concepts part covered "what an ontology is", this part covers what practitioners always ask next: are there real industry cases, does it pay (ROI), and how do you build it and who owns it. The skeleton is the same webinar conversation, reinforced with public material.

Where it is used — cases by industry

The two practitioners in the conversation agree on the conditions. Ontologies earn their keep where there are many systems, the relationships between entities are complex, and decisions span multiple departments. The cases follow those conditions.

  • Finance — regulations change once or twice a week, and finding which internal systems each change touches is a real cost. Ontologies are used to trace quickly which systems an incoming unstructured regulation change affects, and for insurance claim adjudication and underwriting, where you must go beyond clause search and trace the grounds of a judgment.
  • Manufacturing · equipment — root-cause analysis of equipment failures. Vector search over maintenance history, work logs, manuals, and incident reports surfaces similar cases, but a question like "if we close this valve, which pumps, heat exchangers, and reactors does it reach?" is a causal chain that needs an ontology to answer precisely. Also semiconductor risk assessment (simulating in advance which hazard factors a design change touches) and quality history tracing (QMS) — tracking the cycle from customer complaint to factory floor to product change and back to customer satisfaction.
  • SCM — when inventory rises, was it a demand shift, a supplier's lead time, or a planner's strategic call? Customers, suppliers, production, purchasing, and sales are all entangled, so cause analysis needs the relationship structure.

The conversation places the end of this road at the digital twin — before you can simulate design changes digitally, you first move real-world entities and relationships into structure, and that structure is the ontology. Palantir, which commercialized this pattern, defines its ontology as exactly that: "the digital twin of an organization".

Why it pays — ROI in three stages

The conversation lays out ROI as an evolution in three stages.

  • Operating-cost savings — the intuitive early-stage effect. In one case from the conversation, the time spent looking up and reviewing existing material at the design-verification stage (about 30% of the work) nearly disappeared (per the company's own account).
  • Automation and reuse — once structured, the data gets reused across other work and the scope of automation widens.
  • Changing revenue-linked work itself — yield calculation, production-system redesign: the work tied directly to revenue gets restructured.

The consulting-side view adds an axis: decision speed and quality. A company that decides once every two days and one that can decide every ten minutes differ in opportunity cost — count the opportunities you used to miss, and the ROI range grows much larger than the cost savings. Traceable grounds sit on the same axis: when an LLM's answer cannot be backed up, practitioners use it only as reference and go find the grounds themselves; an ontology attaches a traceable trail — this answer came through these entities and these relationships.

How to build — four steps, and spend your time on verification

Everyone does the first three steps — success splits on how systematically you run the fourth.

The steps themselves are ordinary: pick the work the problem lives in, select the data, structure it semantically. What the conversation stresses is the last step — do not skimp on the verification cycle. The final blow against hallucination is evaluation, and each business must build its own machinery for checking, in cycles, whether the answers that come out are actually right: against an answer key when one exists, against the person who knows the answers best when one does not. The conversation's phrase "replace human limits with structure" points at this step.

Staffing runs on three tracks — an ontology engineer, an IT owner who knows the systems and schemas, and domain experts. The weight of that third track is the biggest difference from traditional IT projects: whether the meanings and relationships are right is ultimately a call only someone who knows the business can make. The working style has changed, though — instead of gathering the experts in workshops from day one, the tooling first drafts an ontology from the structured and unstructured data, and the experts verify right/wrong against something concrete. Reviewing a materialized draft is much faster.

Governance — the organization breaks before the technology does

Run the project and the bottleneck turns out to be governance, not technology. The same piece of equipment goes by different names in MES, ERP, and the maintenance system; data ownership is locked per department; KPIs split along departmental goals. The ontology is a frame that absorbs these mismatches technically, but which purpose (KPI) the ontology serves has to be settled by agreement first. The conversation's analogy is a good one — building an ontology resembles executives synthesizing departmental reports into a company-wide judgment. Which is why "everyone just give it a try" without top-level attention rarely works.

  • Ownership — one or two departments (the AI org plus the business owner) carry it at first, but long term the whole workforce comes to share ownership and responsibility.
  • Tacit knowledge — veterans' know-how needs digitizing before it can be ontologized. These days even that step is faster: interview transcripts go into an LLM to draft process maps.
  • A living ontology — it is not built once and done. The structure must update as new data arrives, and above all executions and their after-the-fact evaluations must be stored — which decision ran in which conditions and whether it actually worked, failures included — so the next judgment gets better.
Only when execution results flow back is the ontology "living" — the next problem gets recognized faster.

How to start

  • Small and large in parallel — experience says start small in one work domain and expand stepwise, but large companies run a company-wide multi-year ontology plan alongside the small applications. Pick a first domain that can spread company-wide — where systems are badly disconnected, data is heavily entangled, or the decision structure is clear.
  • Time sense — per the conversation, three months on the short end, up to a year and a half. Some run a PoC first, but companies that have already seen a peer's deployment increasingly go straight to the real project.
  • Work with specialists — as graph DBs and ontologies took off, in-house AI teams often self-study and try small builds, and the conversation's experience is that these rarely work out. When choosing a vendor, look past the signboard at references and the design capability — how they would design an ontology for your business.
  • What you can prepare today — link the documents around your decisions (reports, meeting minutes) to their underlying data and to after-the-fact evaluations. One participant sums this up as "data debt": like technical debt, the company that pays off the debt of context-less, value-only data first pulls ahead. The habit starts with storing temporal and spatial context along with the value — the same 100℃ means different things during normal operation and during maintenance.
In one line Cases cluster where relationships are complex and decisions are at stake; ROI starts as cost savings and grows into decision speed and quality; and the build succeeds or fails on the verification cycle and governance. An ontology is not a finished product — it is a structure that grows by feeding on execution and evaluation.
