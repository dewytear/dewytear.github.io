Levels 3–5 — Meaning, Relations, a Brain OS

Levels 1 and 2 mostly need the words to match. But we often search with words different from the ones we remember. So Level 3 searches by meaning. That is semantic search, and its engine is the vector database.

Keyword search vs semantic search

The biggest difference is "is it the same word (=)" versus "is the meaning close (≈)".

Keyword search finds only the X that equals X; semantic search also finds the Y and Z close in meaning to X.

How does it search by "meaning" — embeddings and chunking

The principle goes like this. A document is split into small pieces (chunks), and each piece goes into an embedding model. The embedding model turns that piece into a coordinate (vector) in "meaning space." Pieces close in meaning gather near each other.

Pieces about the company sit over here, pieces about finance over there — meaning decides the spot. The cluster view in a tool like Qdrant is a visualization of that mass of coordinates.
Term note — Qdrant A leading vector DB that stores and searches vectors is Qdrant. It groups images and text by semantic similarity and shows them as clusters (that demo where "similar pictures bunch together"). Beyond it, Pinecone and Supabase are also used to implement semantic search.

The decisive limit — a chunk cannot see the "whole context"

People mistake a vector DB for "magic that pulls out anything exactly." That is not true. Search only pulls in a few similar chunks, so it is weak on questions that require seeing everything.

SituationWhat vector search doesWhy it is wrong
"Summarize the March 5 meeting"Retrieves only 5 chunks similar to "March 5 meeting summary"If the meeting was split into 20 chunks, it never sees the other 15, so key points go missing
"Which was the highest-revenue week?"Looks at only some chunks (say week 6) and answers "week 6 was the best"In reality weeks 14 and 19 were higher, but it never looked at those chunks
For questions that need the whole thing, it is more accurate to read that markdown in full and answer from it.
So when do vectors shine When you pull a very specific snippet out of a huge body of text. Example: there are 1,000 rules and you ask "what was rule 17 again?" — no need to read everything, just hand over the chunks around number 17. To push accuracy higher, attach metadata or use the hybrid search below.

Raising accuracy — hybrid search and reranking (one step further)

  • Hybrid search — combines semantic (meaning) and BM25 (keyword) scores so each covers the other's weakness. (BM25 also comes up in the LLM Wiki practice chapter's Query.)
  • Reranking — after candidates are picked, a more precise model reorders them.
  • Chunking strategy — how finely you split and how much you overlap drives the quality of the results.
Not everything has to be vectors You do not need to turn the whole Second Brain into vectors. For instance you can keep only the YouTube subtitle folder in a vector DB and leave context/, projects/, and decisions.md as plain markdown. Pick a different approach per folder depending on the kind of data and how it is used. Next comes Level 4 — linking by relationship.

Level 4 · Linking by relationship (knowledge graph)

Where Level 3 found "things close in meaning," Level 4 follows "what is tied to what, and how." That is the knowledge graph. The question takes the shape "start at X and trace the connections through to A." It is usually the most powerful but also the most complex and costly stage.

Entities + relationships = a graph

A knowledge graph is made of two things: entities (people, companies, products) and the named relationships between them (who works where, what endorses what). Unlike wiki links, the relationship carries meaning (a type).

You assemble the answer by following a chain of relationships, as in "Jordan → Acme → Postpilot → Cadently."
It can be lighter than a wiki A wiki makes you read related pages in full (as in "reading the entire AI video production document when all you need is 11Labs"). A graph only needs you to touch the edges (relationships) you need, so in such cases it is actually lighter.

Tools — LightRAG · Graphiti · GraphRAG

Usually software extracts the entities and relationships automatically and builds the graph for you. The notable ones:

ToolCharacteristics
LightRAGLightweight RAG combining graph and vectors. Strong at incremental updates (open source).
GraphitiA knowledge graph framework that also captures time (change).
GraphRAG (Microsoft)An approach that builds entities, relationships, and summaries from a body of documents.

※ The lecture's "graphity-fire" appears to mean Graphiti, and "quadrant" to mean the Qdrant of Level 3.

The real issue is not search but "getting what is in your head out"

The real bottleneck is usually not that the system cannot find things, but that the knowledge in your head has not gone into the system yet.

Use an "interview me (grill me)" style skill to dig into your customers and business and turn it into a file, and that becomes material for the graph and the wiki.

So before blaming the system, ask yourself first: "Are my folders really as holistic as what is in my head?" The habit of feeding in materials, contracts, and subtitles while fleshing them out through brainstorming is the key.

Privacy — where is the data going Processing your data with a Claude model means the content is sent to Anthropic. That may be acceptable for your own work materials, but it is a different story for customer data or sensitive information. In those cases consider running open-source or local models and keeping the Second Brain that holds everything local rather than in the cloud. Being aware yourself of what gets processed where comes first.
Next If you do not need relationships, there is no need to use a graph at all. The final stage, which runs all of this automatically and always, is Level 5 — the always-on brain OS.

Level 5 · The always-on brain OS

The last level takes everything so far (routing·wiki·vectors·graph) and adds "always-on". It is an autonomous brain OS that keeps absorbing and refreshing itself automatically without you having to think about it. The flagship example is GBrain.

What GBrain is A personal knowledge/memory layer built and released as open source by Garry Tan, president and CEO of Y Combinator. It is the operating system he actually runs his own agents (OpenClaw · Hermes) on, and it is said to hold a vast amount of information about pages, people, and companies.

What GBrain combines

PartWhat it is
StorageMarkdown-first + a Postgres backend
RelationsA self-wiring knowledge graph — every time a page is written it extracts entities and creates typed edges such as attended·works_at·invested_in·founded (without an LLM call)
SearchA hybrid of vector + BM25 (keyword) + RRF(rank fusion) + reranking
Always-oncron (scheduled jobs) auto-ingests meetings, email, tweets, and voice memos while you sleep

※ GStack, which comes up alongside it, is a toolkit that lets you use Claude Code like an "AI engineering team (CEO·designer·release manager·QA and so on)".

The always-on loop

The key point is that without a person feeding it every time, the system gathers material by itself, connects relations, reads first before the next answer, and writes back after answering.

A self-refreshing loop that runs without human intervention is the identity of Level 5.
Caution — the too-much-context dilemma "Always-on" has a trap. When there is too much context it gets in the way instead — at what point does the harm outgrow the help? That is why it is safer to control what gets absorbed yourself ("just pull in this week's meeting notes, let's brainstorm together and then put them in together"). Be careful that automation does not turn into noise automation.
Once more — Level 5 is not the best Constant syncing is only worth it when you truly need it (many offline agents, bulk data, team-scale automated pipelines, and so on). The burden of managing cron yourself is heavy too. What you ingest and what you leave matters even more, and the criteria for that call are laid out in Context vs Connections.

All five levels climbed. The final chapter, Applying It, sorts out what to put in, what to leave out, and how to pick your own level.
